# Chapter IX — Elements of Measure Theory

## 1. Measurable spaces

These are detailed solutions to Exercises 1–19. Complements are taken in the relevant ambient space, and $$\sigma(\mathcal S)$$ denotes the $$\sigma$$-algebra generated by $$\mathcal S$$. Inline formulas use GitBook's double-dollar syntax.

### Exercise 1

We verify Examples 1.3(a)–(d).

**(a)** Both $$\{\varnothing,X\}$$ and $$\mathcal P(X)$$ contain $$X$$ and are closed under complements and countable unions: in the first family a union is either empty or $$X$$, while in the second every subset of $$X$$ belongs to the family.

**(b)** Let $$\mathcal C$$ consist of the countable and cocountable subsets of $$X$$. It contains $$X$$ and is closed under complements. For a sequence $$(A_j)$$ in $$\mathcal C$$, if every $$A_j$$ is countable, their union is countable. Otherwise some $$A_k$$ is cocountable, and the complement of the union is contained in the countable set $$A_k^c$$. Thus the union belongs to $$\mathcal C$$.

**(c)** Let $$\mathcal F$$ be the family of finite and cofinite subsets. It contains $$X$$ and is closed under complements. A finite union of finite sets is finite; if one member is cofinite, the union is cofinite. Thus $$\mathcal F$$ is an algebra. If $$X$$ is finite, $$\mathcal F=\mathcal P(X)$$. If $$X$$ is infinite, choose distinct points $$x_0,x_1,\ldots$$. The union of the singletons $$\{x_{2j}\}$$ is infinite, as is its complement, which contains all $$x_{2j+1}$$. Therefore this countable union is not in $$\mathcal F$$, so $$\mathcal F$$ is not a $$\sigma$$-algebra.

**(d)** Let $$\mathcal A_\alpha$$ be $$\sigma$$-algebras over $$X$$. Their intersection contains $$X$$. If a set lies in every $$\mathcal A_\alpha$$, so does its complement. If each member of a sequence lies in every $$\mathcal A_\alpha$$, its union lies in every one of them. Hence the intersection is a $$\sigma$$-algebra.

### Exercise 2

The assertion is false. For example, if $$X=\{1,2\}$$, take $$\mathcal S=\{\{1\}\}$$ and $$\mathcal S'=\{\{2\}\}$$. The two collections differ, but each generates $$\mathcal P(X)$$, since each singleton is the complement of the other.

### Exercise 3

Write $$\mathcal F$$ for the mosaics and call a measurable rectangle a set $$A\times B$$ with $$A\in\mathcal A_1$$ and $$B\in\mathcal A_2$$.

**(a)** The whole product is a rectangle. The intersection of two rectangles is a rectangle, and

$$
(A\times B)^c=(A^c\times X_2)\mathbin{\dot\cup}(A\times B^c).
$$

Consequently the intersection of two mosaics is a finite union of measurable rectangles, and the complement of a mosaic is also a finite union of measurable rectangles, by De Morgan's law and distribution of finite intersections over finite unions. Any finite union of measurable rectangles is a mosaic: collect all first-coordinate sides and their complements, and likewise all second-coordinate sides. The nonempty intersections in each coordinate form finite disjoint measurable partitions; their product rectangles form a finite disjoint partition, and the given union is a union of some of its cells. The empty union is represented by the empty rectangle. Hence mosaics are closed under complements and finite unions, and form an algebra.

**(b)** Every measurable rectangle is itself a mosaic, so $$\mathcal A_1\otimes\mathcal A_2\subset\sigma(\mathcal F)$$. Conversely, every mosaic is a finite union of measurable rectangles and therefore belongs to $$\mathcal A_1\otimes\mathcal A_2$$. Minimality gives the reverse inclusion.

### Exercise 4

**(a)** A point belongs to $$\limsup_j A_j$$ precisely when it belongs to infinitely many $$A_j$$: every tail contains an index at which the point occurs. It belongs to $$\liminf_j A_j$$ precisely when it belongs to all $$A_j$$ from some index onward.

**(b)** Eventual membership implies infinitely many occurrences, so always

$$
\liminf_{j\to\infty} A_j\subset \limsup_{j\to\infty} A_j.
$$

The reverse inclusion can fail: take a nonempty $$X$$ and alternate $$A_{2j}=X$$ and $$A_{2j+1}=\varnothing$$. Then the limsup is $$X$$ and the liminf is empty.

### Exercise 5

**(a)** If $$A_j\subset A_{j+1}$$, then for every $$n$$, the union of the tail is $$\bigcup_j A_j$$ and the intersection of the tail is $$A_n$$. Thus both limits equal $$\bigcup_j A_j$$. If the sequence decreases, every tail intersection is $$\bigcap_j A_j$$ and the tail union is $$A_n$$; both limits equal $$\bigcap_j A_j$$.

**(b)** At each $$x$$, the values $$\chi_{A_j}(x)$$ are either zero or one. Such a sequence converges precisely when it is eventually constant. It is eventually one exactly on the liminf, and it is eventually zero exactly outside the limsup. Since the liminf is contained in the limsup, equality of the two means eventual constancy at every point; the limiting value is then $$\chi_A$$, where $$A$$ is their common value. Conversely, pointwise convergence to $$\chi_A$$ makes membership eventually constant at every point, with eventual membership exactly on $$A$$, so both set limits equal $$A$$.

### Exercise 6

**(a)** Necessity is immediate because $$\mathcal S\subset\mathcal B$$. For the converse define

$$
\mathcal D=\{B\subset Y:f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal A\}.
$$

Inverse images preserve the whole space, complements, and countable unions, so $$\mathcal D$$ is a $$\sigma$$-algebra. If inverse images of all sets in $$\mathcal S$$ belong to $$\mathcal A$$, then $$\mathcal D$$ contains $$\sigma(\mathcal S)=\mathcal B$$. This is measurability.

**(b)** A continuous map takes inverse images of open subsets of $$Y$$ to open subsets of $$X$$. The open subsets generate $$\mathcal B(Y)$$, so part (a) gives Borel measurability.

### Exercise 7

The trace family $$\mathcal A|_Y=\{A\cap Y:A\in\mathcal A\}$$ contains $$Y=X\cap Y$$. Relative to $$Y$$,

$$
Y\setminus(A\cap Y)=A^c\cap Y,\qquad
\bigcup_j(A_j\cap Y)=\left(\bigcup_j A_j\right)\cap Y.
$$

Both right-hand sides lie in the trace family, so it is a $$\sigma$$-algebra over $$Y$$. The same proof also works when $$Y$$ is empty.

### Exercise 8

**(a)** For every $$C\in\mathcal C$$,

$$
(g\circ f)^{-1}(C)=f^{-1}\bigl(g^{-1}(C)\bigr)\in\mathcal A,
$$

because $$g^{-1}(C)\in\mathcal B$$ and $$f$$ is measurable.

**(b)** For $$B\in\mathcal B$$, the inverse image under the restriction is

$$
(f|_A)^{-1}(B)=A\cap f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal A|_A.
$$

Thus the restriction is measurable for the induced $$\sigma$$-algebra.

### Exercise 9

Let $$i:X\hookrightarrow Y$$ be inclusion. Every open subset of the subspace $$X$$ is $$i^{-1}(U)=U\cap X$$ for some open $$U\subset Y$$. Hence $$\mathcal B(X)\subset\mathcal B(Y)|_X$$, since the latter is a $$\sigma$$-algebra containing all such sets.

Conversely, $$\{B\subset Y:i^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal B(X)\}$$ is a $$\sigma$$-algebra containing every open subset of $$Y$$. It therefore contains $$\mathcal B(Y)$$. Thus every $$B\cap X$$ with $$B\in\mathcal B(Y)$$ is Borel in $$X$$, proving the reverse inclusion.

### Exercise 10

Equivalent metrics induce the same topology. Therefore they have the same open sets and generate the same Borel $$\sigma$$-algebra. Equivalently, the identity maps in both directions are continuous and hence Borel measurable.

### Exercise 11

Each basis element is open, so $$\sigma(\mathcal M)\subset\mathcal B(X)$$. Conversely, any open set is the union of the basis elements contained in it. Since $$\mathcal M$$ is countable, this is a countable union (possibly empty), and belongs to $$\sigma(\mathcal M)$$. Thus every open set, and hence every Borel set, belongs to that $$\sigma$$-algebra.

### Exercise 12

In a Hausdorff space each compact subset is closed, so every compact set is Borel and $$\sigma(\mathcal K)\subset\mathcal B(X)$$. Conversely, let $$U$$ be open and put $$F=X\setminus U$$. Since $$F\cap K_j$$ is closed in the compact set $$K_j$$, it is compact. The assumed exhaustion yields

$$
F=\bigcup_{j=0}^{\infty}(F\cap K_j)\in\sigma(\mathcal K).
$$

Thus $$U\in\sigma(\mathcal K)$$, giving the other inclusion. No nesting of the sets $$K_j$$ is needed.

### Exercise 13

Give $$Z$$ the subspace topology. The product topology on $$X\times Z$$ is the subspace topology inherited from $$X\times Y$$: intersections of basic rectangles $$U\times V$$ with $$X\times Z$$ are precisely the basic rectangles $$U\times(V\cap Z)$$. By Exercise 9,

$$
\mathcal B(X\times Z)=\mathcal B(X\times Y)|_{X\times Z}.
$$

Also $$\mathcal B(Z)=\mathcal B(Y)|_Z$$. Define

$$
\mathcal D=\{M\subset X\times Y:M\cap(X\times Z)\in\mathcal B(X)\otimes\mathcal B(Z)\}.
$$

It is a $$\sigma$$-algebra on $$X\times Y$$, since intersection with $$X\times Z$$ preserves complements relative to the respective spaces and countable unions. It contains each rectangle $$A\times B$$ with $$A\in\mathcal B(X)$$ and $$B\in\mathcal B(Y)$$, because its trace is $$A\times(B\cap Z)$$. Thus it contains $$\mathcal B(X)\otimes\mathcal B(Y)=\mathcal B(X\times Y)$$. The trace identity above now gives $$\mathcal B(X\times Z)\subset\mathcal B(X)\otimes\mathcal B(Z)$$.

For the reverse inclusion, the coordinate projections on $$X\times Z$$ are continuous. Therefore the preimages of Borel sets under them are Borel, and every generating rectangle is Borel. Hence equality holds.

### Exercise 14

For every generating rectangle $$B_1\times B_2$$, with $$B_j\in\mathcal B(Y_j)$$,

$$
(f_1\times f_2)^{-1}(B_1\times B_2)
=f_1^{-1}(B_1)\times f_2^{-1}(B_2)
\in\mathcal B(X_1)\otimes\mathcal B(X_2).
$$

By the generator criterion in Exercise 6, the inverse image of every member of $$\mathcal B(Y_1)\otimes\mathcal B(Y_2)$$ lies in $$\mathcal B(X_1)\otimes\mathcal B(X_2)$$. This argument does not require the product Borel and product $$\sigma$$-algebras to coincide.

### Exercise 15

The family of rectangles $$\mathcal A\times\mathcal A$$ is a $$\sigma$$-algebra **if and only if** $$A=\varnothing$$ or $$A=X$$. In either case $$\mathcal A=\{\varnothing,X\}$$, and the rectangle family is $$\{\varnothing,X\times X\}$$.

Otherwise choose $$a\in A$$ and $$b\in A^c$$. If the complement of $$A\times A$$ were a rectangle $$C\times D$$, its membership would include both $$(a,b)$$ and $$(b,a)$$. It would then also contain $$(a,a)$$, a contradiction. Thus the family fails closure under complements.

### Exercise 16

Set

$$
\mathcal D=\bigcup\{\sigma(\mathcal C):\mathcal C\subset\mathcal S\text{ is countable}\}.
$$

Each $$\sigma(\mathcal C)$$ is contained in $$\sigma(\mathcal S)$$, so $$\mathcal D\subset\sigma(\mathcal S)$$. The family $$\mathcal D$$ contains $$X$$ and is closed under complements. If $$D_j\in\mathcal D$$, select countable $$\mathcal C_j\subset\mathcal S$$ with $$D_j\in\sigma(\mathcal C_j)$$. The union $$\mathcal C=\bigcup_j\mathcal C_j$$ is countable and all $$D_j$$ lie in $$\sigma(\mathcal C)$$; hence their union lies in $$\mathcal D$$. Thus $$\mathcal D$$ is a $$\sigma$$-algebra. It contains every $$S\in\mathcal S$$, since $$S\in\sigma(\{S\})$$, and therefore contains $$\sigma(\mathcal S)$$. The formula also covers $$\mathcal S=\varnothing$$ by allowing $$\mathcal C=\varnothing$$.

### Exercise 17

**(i)** Let $$\mathcal A_A=\{B\subset X:A\subset B\text{ or }A\subset B^c\}$$. It contains $$X$$, and taking complements exchanges the two alternatives. For a sequence $$(B_j)$$ in the family, if some $$B_j$$ contains $$A$$, then so does the union. Otherwise every $$B_j$$ is disjoint from $$A$$, so their union is disjoint from $$A$$. Hence $$\mathcal A_A$$ is a $$\sigma$$-algebra. This includes the cases $$A=\varnothing$$ and $$A=X$$.

**(ii)** If $$\mathcal S\subset\mathcal A_A$$, minimality of the generated $$\sigma$$-algebra gives $$\sigma(\mathcal S)\subset\mathcal A_A$$.

### Exercise 18

Suppose $$\Delta_X\in\mathcal B(X)\otimes\mathcal B(X)$$. The product $$\sigma$$-algebra is generated by open rectangles: the open sets generate each factor, and inverse images under the coordinate projections show that the resulting $$\sigma$$-algebra contains every Borel rectangle. By Exercise 16, there are countably many open rectangles $$U_k\times V_k$$ whose generated $$\sigma$$-algebra contains $$\Delta_X$$. Put $$\mathcal S_1=\{U_k:k\in\mathbb N\}$$ and $$\mathcal S_2=\{V_k:k\in\mathbb N\}$$, and let $$\mathcal A_i=\sigma(\mathcal S_i)$$. Then $$\Delta_X\in\mathcal A_1\otimes\mathcal A_2$$. By measurability of sections, for each $$x\in X$$ the section

$$
\Delta_X^{[x]}=\{y\in X:(y,x)\in\Delta_X\}=\{x\}
$$

belongs to $$\mathcal A_1$$.

Define

$$
\phi(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}3^{-k-1}\chi_{U_k}(x)\in\mathbb R.
$$

If two points first differ in membership at index $$m$$, the difference in that term has magnitude $$3^{-m-1}$$, whereas the sum of magnitudes of all later terms is $$\tfrac12 3^{-m-1}$$. Hence their values of $$\phi$$ differ. If $$\phi(x)=\phi(y)$$, the points therefore have identical membership in every $$U_k$$. With $$A=\{x,y\}$$, all generators belong to $$\mathcal A_A$$; Exercise 17 implies that all sets of $$\mathcal A_1$$ either contain both points or contain neither. Since $$\{x\}\in\mathcal A_1$$, this forces $$x=y$$. Thus $$\phi:X\to\mathbb R$$ is injective.

### Exercise 19

Let $$X=\mathcal P(\mathbb R)$$ with any Hausdorff topology. Its diagonal is closed in $$X\times X$$: if $$x\ne y$$, disjoint neighborhoods $$U$$ of $$x$$ and $$V$$ of $$y$$ give a product neighborhood $$U\times V$$ disjoint from the diagonal. Consequently $$\Delta_X\in\mathcal B(X\times X)$$.

If the product Borel $$\sigma$$-algebra equaled $$\mathcal B(X)\otimes\mathcal B(X)$$, Exercise 18 would give an injection $$\mathcal P(\mathbb R)\to\mathbb R$$. Cantor's theorem rules this out: such an injection, together with the singleton injection $$\mathbb R\to\mathcal P(\mathbb R)$$, would give a bijection by Cantor–Bernstein. Therefore

$$
\mathcal B(X)\otimes\mathcal B(X)\subsetneq\mathcal B(X\times X).
$$
